The research side of this site is about getting an answer you can trust. This side is about something harder: getting a room full of people to believe a result they could have derived themselves. Every instrument here is built so a student can attack the model directly — move the curve, flip the sign, break the conclusion — instead of watching it be asserted on a slide.

Market Forces Lab

Eight rooms covering the first arc of any introductory microeconomics course: demand and supply, what shifts a curve versus what merely moves along one, equilibrium and how it repairs itself, elasticity and its determinants, the elasticity–revenue relationship, consumer and producer surplus, and deadweight loss. Students play; the diagrams respond; every claim in the lecture becomes something they can falsify in about four seconds.

Built against Mankiw's Principles of Microeconomics (Ch. 4–7) and Baye & Prince's Managerial Economics and Business Strategy (Ch. 2–3) · basic calculus only · ungraded, nothing is submitted or collected

01Shift or Slide?Eighteen scenarios. Does a curve move, or do we travel along one that didn't? Timed, scored, keyboard-playable.
02Rooftop Solar SandboxNine switches, one per non-price determinant. Post your own price and watch the shortage or glut appear.
03Elasticity DuelTwo goods, both prices up 20%. Call the more elastic one, then name which determinant explains it.
04The Pricing RoomRun a price test on a real revenue curve. Midpoint method computed live; gained and lost revenue shaded.
05Two Ways to Shrink a MarketSqueeze supply or squeeze demand. With inelastic demand only one of them works — find where the verdict flips.
06The Refurbished Laptop FloorFour named buyers, four named sellers. Per-person surplus, marginal trader highlighted.
07The Quantity DialDial output by hand and watch value that never got created. Deadweight loss in both directions.
08Arguments Worth HavingTwelve open questions with no answer key. Surge caps, export bans, zero-marginal-cost supply curves.

Market Maker

Do the Lab tutorial round first — this level assumes it. Twelve questions drawn from a rotating pool, in three escalating tiers, with a clock on every one and a streak multiplier that rewards not breaking. Answer fast and you keep more of the points; get one wrong and the multiplier resets to zero, but the run never ends early — everybody finishes all twelve. It reaches past the lab into price ceilings and floors, tax incidence, and opportunity and sunk cost.

TIER 1Recognise100 base points. Does the curve shift or do we move along it? Normal or inferior? Elastic or not?
TIER 2Apply200 base points. Compute a midpoint elasticity, find an equilibrium, size a shortage, click the right region on a diagram.
TIER 3Judge350 base points. Traps, ambiguous two-shift cases, and the questions where the confident answer is the wrong one.
BONUSSpeed & streakUp to +50% for answering quickly. Three in a row ×1.2, five ×1.5, eight ×2.
AFTERConcept diagnosticsAccuracy tracked across eleven concepts, so you can see which idea is quietly costing you points.
ALWAYSNo marksCarries no weight, affects nothing on your transcript. It exists because replaying beats rereading.

Entry is gated and honest about it. You enter a roll number or institute email and choose a display name before the floor opens. That identifies your run when you send it in — it is a name tag, not a password, and the page says so. Scores, streaks and progress live only in your own browser while you play. Nothing leaves your device unless you press Submit at the end of a run, and what gets sent goes to your instructor alone. No other student sees your score.

How to use these in a course

Three patterns that work · pick one, they are not cumulative

  • Cold open. Put Room 1 on the projector before you have taught anything and let the class shout answers. The scenarios they get wrong tell you exactly which half of your lecture to spend the time on.
  • Post the link, say nothing. Drop the URL into your LMS as optional practice. Nothing is graded or collected, so the only students who open it are the ones who want to — which is precisely the signal you cannot get from an assignment.
  • Run it as a five-minute opener. Market Maker takes about six minutes. Start class with it, let the room compare scores out loud, then teach into whichever concept the diagnostics flagged. The submissions arrive in your response sheet while you are still talking.
  • Argue from a diagram. Room 5 produces a result most students refuse to believe: a crackdown that reduces quantity and raises total spending. Project it, let them dispute it, then hand them the elasticity slider and let them find the condition under which they are right.

Everything runs client-side in the browser. There is no backend and no login anywhere here. The Market Forces Lab collects nothing at all — no analytics, no student data of any kind, and progress is remembered only in that browser's own storage. Market Maker is the one exception, and only by the student's own action: a run stays in the browser unless the student presses Submit at the end, which sends that one result to the instructor's own Google Form and nowhere else. Details on the privacy page.

Adopting these elsewhere. Instructors at any institution are welcome to link to these directly, or to take the source and retarget it. The pages are plain HTML with no build step: the question bank lives in its own file, so the subject matter can be swapped without touching the game engine, and a single config file sets the run length, timers, points and where submissions go. Nothing in them is specific to this institute.
